# **Jebena Bun: The Art of the Coffee Ceremony**
### **The Soul of the Household**
While tea is the drink of the bustling streets, **Jebena Bun** (Coffee from the clay pot) is the heart of the home. In Jigjiga, the coffee ceremony is a daily ritual—usually performed in the late afternoon—that brings family, neighbors, and friends together. It is a time to slow down, talk about the day, and honor the guest.
### **The Ritual: Step-by-Step**
The ceremony follows a traditional process that hasn't changed for centuries. It is usually performed by the woman of the house, who takes great pride in the quality of her brew.
 1. **The Roasting:** Green coffee beans are washed and placed on a flat iron pan over a small charcoal stove (*Buraaj*). They are stirred until they turn dark, oily, and aromatic.
 2. **The Scent of Uunsi:** While the beans roast, a small piece of **Uunsi** (frankincense or myrrh) is placed on the coals. The sweet, earthy smoke fills the room, signaling to neighbors that coffee is being prepared.
 3. **The Grinding:** The roasted beans are ground by hand using a mortar and pestle (*Madiqa*). The rhythmic "thump-thump" of the grinding is part of the ceremony’s music.
 4. **The Brewing:** The ground coffee is added to the **Jebena**—a beautiful, handcrafted clay pot with a spherical base and a long neck. Water is added, and the pot is placed back on the coals to boil.
 5. **The Pouring:** Once the coffee has settled, it is poured from a height into tiny ceramic cups called **Sini**. The height of the pour creates a light froth on top, which is a sign of a master brewer.
### **The Three Rounds**
A true ceremony isn't over after one cup! Traditionally, there are three rounds of coffee served, each getting slightly weaker as more water is added to the pot:
 * **Awel:** The first round—strong and bold.
 * **Tani:** The second round.
 * **Baraka:** The third and final round (the word means "blessing"). It is considered good luck to stay for all three.
### **What to Eat with Your Coffee**
In Jigjiga, coffee is never served alone. It is almost always accompanied by a snack to balance the bitterness of the dark roast:
 * **Popcorn (Bilis):** The most common companion to coffee.
 * **Koolo:** Roasted barley or grains mixed with spices.
 * **Fried Snacks:** Like Sambuus or small sweet fritters.

### **Quick Facts Sidebar:**
 * **The Pot:** The **Jebena** is made of black clay and is often decorated with intricate patterns.
 * **The Flavor:** Expect a deep, smoky, and earthy flavor. It is often served with a spoon of sugar but never with milk.
 * **The Etiquette:** It is polite to smell the roasting beans when they are passed around the room before they are ground.
 * **The Symbolism:** A steaming Jebena on a table is a universal sign of "Welcome" in the Somali Region.
